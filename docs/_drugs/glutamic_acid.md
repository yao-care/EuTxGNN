## Glutamic Acid: From Maple Syrup Urine Disease to Postmenopausal Osteoporosis
{: .text-alpha }

## One-Sentence Summary

Glutamic acid (marketed as Maapliv) was originally authorised in the EU for acute decompensation episodes of maple syrup urine disease (MSUD) in patients who cannot take an oral or enteral BCAA-free formulation.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **postmenopausal osteoporosis**, but there are **0 registered clinical trials** and only **indirect literature** (one animal study and one small human calcium-absorption study of a polymer derivative) supporting this direction.
This is a computational prediction with very limited evidence.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available. Glutamic acid is a naturally occurring amino acid, and its authorised use in MSUD is metabolic and nutritional in nature. No direct mechanistic bridge from that indication to bone metabolism can be drawn from the data compiled for this report.

Indirect support is weak. In ovariectomized mice, glutamic acid ameliorated estrogen deficiency-induced menopausal-like symptoms (PMID 26144993). A small human study found that poly-gamma-glutamic acid, a polymer derivative rather than glutamic acid itself, acutely enhanced calcium absorption in postmenopausal women (PMID 18187428). Neither study measured bone endpoints such as bone mineral density or fractures for glutamic acid itself.

Several other retrieved papers concern vitamin K2 or D3, whose effects involve glutamic acid residues in bone proteins, as well as genetic or metabolomic associations. These do not test glutamic acid as a treatment. The very high TxGNN score is a computational prediction only and should not be read as evidence of efficacy.

- **Drug Interactions** (5 moderate-level interactions): amphetamine, benzphetamine, dextroamphetamine, lisdexamfetamine and metamfetamine.

Several of these signals, especially kidney disease, nephrolithiasis and calcium disorders, overlap with comorbidities common in an osteoporosis population and warrant careful review. Please refer to the SmPC for label-based warnings and contraindications.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The prediction rests on a computational score alone. There are no registered clinical trials, the only supportive literature is one animal study and one acute calcium-absorption study of a polymer derivative, and no mechanism links the original MSUD indication to bone metabolism.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data and a plausible pathway linking glutamic acid to bone remodelling
- Label-based warnings and contraindications from the SmPC
- Preclinical studies of glutamic acid itself with bone endpoints (bone mineral density, turnover markers)
- Safety evaluation covering renal function, calcium handling and cardiovascular risk in postmenopausal women
- Assessment of formulation and route, since the original product was an intravenous acute-care formulation
