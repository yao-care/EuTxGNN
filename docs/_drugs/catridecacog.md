# Catridecacog: From Congenital Factor XIII A-Subunit Deficiency to Primary Release Disorder of Platelets

## One-Sentence Summary

Catridecacog is a recombinant factor XIII A-subunit, originally used for long-term prevention of bleeding in patients with congenital factor XIII A-subunit deficiency.
The TxGNN model's top-ranked prediction is **primary release disorder of platelets**, but **no clinical trials or publications** support this specific prediction.
The only prediction with substantial evidence (**7 clinical trials, 3 publications**) is congenital factor XIII deficiency, which is the drug's already-approved use and therefore not a true repurposing candidate.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on known information, catridecacog is a recombinant form of the factor XIII A-subunit. It replaces the missing enzyme (a transglutaminase), allowing fibrin strands to cross-link and stabilise the blood clot. Its efficacy in congenital factor XIII A-subunit deficiency is established.

The predicted new indication, primary release disorder of platelets, is a different kind of bleeding disorder. It arises from intrinsic defects in platelet granule release and secretion, not from a lack of clot-stabilising enzyme. Factor XIII replacement acts downstream of the platelet defect and is not expected to correct it. The high TxGNN score is therefore best read as a computational association within the "bleeding disorder" category, not as evidence of a mechanistic fit.

The other top-ranked predictions show the same pattern. Examples are pseudo-von Willebrand disease, Glanzmann thrombasthenia, Scott syndrome, and hemophilia. All lack supporting trials or literature, and their underlying defects are not addressed by factor XIII replacement. One exception is worth noting: acquired coagulation factor deficiency (rank 7) has a plausible rationale, because acquired factor XIII deficiency could in theory respond to replacement. That rationale is unproven and is better treated as a research question than a development candidate.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The top-ranked prediction (primary release disorder of platelets) has no trial or literature support, and its underlying platelet defect is not one that factor XIII replacement would be expected to correct. The one well-supported prediction, congenital factor XIII deficiency, is the drug's existing approved use, not a new indication. The sole EU authorization is cancelled, so availability would also need to be confirmed.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Detailed mechanism of action data to support or refute any mechanistic link to platelet disorders
- Safety information from the SmPC (warnings, contraindications, interactions)
- Preclinical or mechanistic studies testing whether factor XIII replacement affects bleeding in platelet function disorders
- Confirmation of current product availability, given the cancelled EU authorization
- If pursued at all, a dedicated hypothesis-driven study of acquired factor XIII deficiency (rank 7), noting that catridecacog covers only A-subunit deficiency
