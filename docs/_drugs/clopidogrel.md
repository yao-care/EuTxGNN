# Clopidogrel: From Atherothrombotic Event Prevention to Migraine Disorder

## One-Sentence Summary

Clopidogrel is an antiplatelet drug originally used to prevent atherothrombotic events after myocardial infarction, ischaemic stroke, peripheral arterial disease and acute coronary syndrome.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **migraine disorder**, the best-supported of its top predictions,
with **6 related clinical trials** and **20 publications** currently supporting this direction (the evidence is concentrated in patients with patent foramen ovale or after septal closure).

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Clopidogrel blocks the P2Y12 receptor on platelets and so reduces platelet aggregation. Detailed mechanism-of-action data is not available in the data compiled for this report, but this P2Y12 antiplatelet action is well established.

Platelet activation and paradoxical microemboli (small clots crossing from the venous to the arterial side, typically through a patent foramen ovale, PFO) may contribute to migraine, particularly migraine with aura. Platelet-derived vasoactive substances such as serotonin may also trigger cortical spreading depression. If so, blocking platelet aggregation could plausibly reduce attacks. Preclinical papers also link P2Y12 receptors to migraine models in mice.

The link between the original indication (preventing vascular and thrombotic events) and migraine is therefore the shared platelet/thrombotic pathway, rather than a similar disease. The caveat is that most supporting evidence comes from people with PFO or from patients after atrial septal defect closure. It is not yet clear that the benefit extends to migraine patients in general, and results across studies are heterogeneous.

## Safety Considerations

- **Disease-level contraindication signals (knowledge graph, not label text):** acute liver failure, hepatitis, blood coagulation disease, injury, thrombocytopenic purpura, active peptic ulcer disease, intracranial haemorrhage.
- **Drug Interactions (100 recorded; main ones):**
  - Major: omeprazole, esomeprazole, rabeprazole (proton pump inhibitors, which can reduce clopidogrel activation), pioglitazone, loperamide, repaglinide
  - Moderate: acetylsalicylic acid (bleeding risk when combined), bupropion, morphine, cimetidine, clarithromycin, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, among others
- **Bleeding risk** is a recognised concern and, together with the lack of confirmatory Phase 3 data, limits a stronger recommendation. Migraine patients are often otherwise healthy, so the benefit-risk balance needs particular care.

Please also refer to the SmPC for full warnings and contraindications.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Proceed with Guardrails**

**Rationale:**
Several randomised studies (CANOA, a pilot RCT, and a recently published multicentre RCT) and a completed Phase 2 trial support a benefit of clopidogrel-based antiplatelet therapy in migraine, but mainly in people with PFO or after septal closure. Results are heterogeneous and there is no confirmatory Phase 3 evidence in the general migraine population, so progression should be limited to a research setting with bleeding-risk controls.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Review the final results of COMPETE (NCT05546320) and the 2026 BMJ randomised trial before advancing
- Confirmatory Phase 3 evidence, ideally including migraine patients without PFO
- Detailed mechanism-of-action data (MOA)
- Bleeding-risk assessment and a patient-selection plan (for example, PFO or right-to-left shunt confirmed by imaging)
- A drug-interaction management plan, especially for proton pump inhibitors and aspirin
- Full review of the SmPC warnings and contraindications
- Clarification of the market situation, since all 12 EU centralised authorisations in the data compiled are cancelled and a new route to supply would need to be established
