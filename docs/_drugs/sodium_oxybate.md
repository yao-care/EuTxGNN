## Sodium Oxybate: From Narcolepsy with Cataplexy to Insomnia
{: .text-alpha }

## One-Sentence Summary

Sodium oxybate (Xyrem) was originally authorised in the EU for narcolepsy with cataplexy in adults.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **insomnia**, but the supporting evidence is limited to **3 clinical trials** (only one directly on-indication, a small Phase 2 study) and a literature set made up mostly of reviews and narcolepsy studies.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on the published literature, sodium oxybate (gamma-hydroxybutyrate, GHB) acts as an agonist at GABA-B and GHB receptors. It is reported to increase slow-wave sleep and consolidate sleep architecture, which is mechanistically plausible for insomnia.

Narcolepsy involves disrupted nocturnal sleep as well as daytime sleepiness. Sodium oxybate's benefit on night-time sleep in narcolepsy, together with open-label sleep data in Parkinson disease, offers an indirect link to insomnia. This remains a hypothesis. The product is labelled for narcolepsy, not insomnia, and no Phase 3 confirmation exists.

Other predicted indications are much weaker. "Sleep disorder, initiating and maintaining sleep" is essentially the same clinical entity as insomnia. The remaining predictions (for example restless legs syndrome, ADHD and several rare diseases) have no supporting trials, and one literature item suggests restless legs syndrome may worsen with sodium oxybate.

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindications suggested by the knowledge graph** (disease associations in the TxGNN knowledge graph, not label text): hypertension, epilepsy, anxiety and neurotic disorders, dysthymic disorder, liver disease, gallbladder disease, substance abuse or dependence, obstructive sleep apnoea and other sleep apnoea syndromes, congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, respiratory failure, succinic semialdehyde dehydrogenase deficiency and parasomnia.
- **Drug Interactions**: 48 interactions were identified. Major interactions include:
  - Ethanol
  - Opioids (morphine, codeine, hydrocodone, opium)
  - Sedating antihistamines (promethazine, chlorpheniramine, brompheniramine, carbinoxamine, acrivastine)
  - Cannabinoids (dronabinol, nabilone)
  - Bupropion, fenfluramine, metoclopramide and sibutramine
  
  Moderate interactions include diazoxide, amyl nitrite and phentolamine.
- **Other concerns from the literature**: abuse potential, central nervous system depression, and reports of sleep-related eating disorder and restless legs symptoms during treatment.

Please refer to the SmPC for formal warnings and contraindications.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The only directly relevant evidence is one small Phase 2 insomnia trial (n=48) with no Phase 3 confirmation. The EU authorisation has been cancelled. The central nervous system depressant risk, abuse potential, many major interactions and several knowledge-graph contraindications (including sleep apnoea and respiratory failure) call for strict guardrails. Insomnia is a large population, so the risk-benefit balance must be well established first.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Results and full analysis of NCT00383643, followed by an adequately powered Phase 3 insomnia programme
- Detailed mechanism of action data
- Warnings and contraindications from the SmPC
- A risk-management plan covering abuse, respiratory depression, and co-medication with alcohol, opioids and sedatives
- Confirmation of the regulatory and supply route, given that the EU authorisation is cancelled
