# Colesevelam: From Primary Hypercholesterolaemia to Gout

## One-Sentence Summary

Colesevelam is a bile acid sequestrant, originally authorised in the EU (as Cholestagel) to lower LDL cholesterol in adults with primary hypercholesterolaemia.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be useful for **gout**, but **no clinical trials and no publications** currently support this direction.
The prediction is based on the model alone.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available. Based on known information, colesevelam is a non-absorbed bile acid sequestrant. Its efficacy in primary hypercholesterolaemia is established, and it lowers total cholesterol and LDL-C.

A possible link to gout is that altered lipid and bile acid metabolism is associated with metabolic syndrome and hyperuricaemia. This link is speculative. Colesevelam has no established uric acid-lowering mechanism, and the high TxGNN score is likely driven by network associations rather than a demonstrated pharmacological pathway.

For context, other predictions for this drug have somewhat more biological plausibility. Diabetic nephropathy is the most plausible candidate among the top ten, and congestive heart failure has an indirect link through risk factors. Neither is the focus of this report.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The TxGNN score is high, but there are no trials or publications for gout, and colesevelam has no known uric acid-lowering mechanism. The only authorisation on record is cancelled, so the drug is not currently marketed in the EU under this licence.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data for colesevelam, and any plausible mechanistic link to urate metabolism
- A systematic literature search for colesevelam and serum uric acid or gout outcomes
- Label warnings and contraindications from the SmPC
- Confirmation of current EU availability, given that the only authorisation is cancelled
- Consideration of better-supported candidates such as diabetic nephropathy, which would still need direct clinical data
