# Tryptophan: From Maple Syrup Urine Disease to Restless Legs Syndrome

## One-Sentence Summary

Tryptophan (marketed as Maapliv) was originally authorised in the EU for acute decompensation episodes of maple syrup urine disease (MSUD).
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **Restless Legs Syndrome**, but there are **no registered clinical trials** and only **6 publications**, mostly older or indirect, supporting this direction.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available. Based on known information, tryptophan is an essential amino acid and the precursor of serotonin and melatonin. Its authorised use is in the metabolic disease MSUD, where it is part of an amino acid-based therapy, and mechanistically it may be applicable to RLS through its effects on neurotransmitter pathways.

The link to restless legs syndrome rests on the idea that serotonergic and dopaminergic signalling are involved in RLS and periodic limb movements. Small clinical reports from 1986–1987 examined L-tryptophan in RLS and periodic leg movements. Pharmacovigilance data also show that serotonergic antidepressants can worsen movement disorders, so the serotonergic system is clearly relevant, but the direction of effect is not straightforward.

The original indication (a metabolic disorder) and RLS are not closely related clinically. The prediction is therefore a hypothesis-generating signal, not a confirmed therapeutic link.

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindication signals from the knowledge graph (not label text)**: autoimmune disease, systemic lupus erythematosus (including paediatric), anxiety disorder, neurotic disorder, dysthymic disorder, schizophrenia, phenylketonuria and other disorders of phenylalanine metabolism, and eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome (including the form associated with tryptophan). The eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome link reflects a historical outbreak linked to contaminated L-tryptophan supplements.
- **Drug Interactions**: 17 interactions were identified.
  - Major (10): lorcaserin, dolasetron, palonosetron, ondansetron, fenfluramine, granisetron, sibutramine, methylene blue, doxepin (including topical), procarbazine. Many of these involve serotonergic effects, so serotonin syndrome is a key concern.
  - Moderate (7): morphine, codeine, hydrocodone, cyproheptadine, ozanimod, ifosfamide.

Please refer to the SmPC for official warnings and contraindications.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The RLS prediction has a very high model score, but the supporting evidence is limited to two small, dated clinical reports (1986–1987), with no registered trials. The product's EU authorisation is cancelled. Many serotonergic drug interactions, and the history of eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome, call for caution. At best this is a research question.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Full text and critical appraisal of the 1986–1987 clinical studies, as no abstracts were available
- A modern, controlled trial design for tryptophan in RLS
- Mechanism of action data
- SmPC warnings and contraindications (the package insert is not yet parsed)
- Confirmation of product quality and purity requirements, given the eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome history
- A plan for managing serotonergic drug interactions
- Clarification of why the authorisation was cancelled and what formulation would be used for RLS (route and dosage form compatibility not yet assessed)
